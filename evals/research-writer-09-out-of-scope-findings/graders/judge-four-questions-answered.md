The plan limits the request to England, 1905 to 1914, and asks four questions. The findings answer each from inside those limits:
1. How did people use penny-farthings for everyday travel in English towns, such as riding to work? (Findings: in April 1908 the Coventry Evening Herald printed a photograph of watchmaker Albert Rowe riding his penny-farthing to his workshop on Hertford Street; the caption said he rode it there every working day.)
2. Who rode them? (Findings: mostly men who had learned on the machine in their youth; the Ordinary Riders' Club's 1908 run through Coventry had 14 riders, average age 58.)
3. How did a rider get on and off? (Findings: from behind, left foot on a small step just above the back wheel, push off with the right foot, stand on the step and slide forward into the saddle; the same in reverse to get off.)
4. What were the dangers? (Findings: the rider sat almost above the front wheel's hub, so a stone or rut that stopped the wheel could throw him over the handlebars head first, "taking a header"; a 1908 handbook told riders to hook their legs over the handlebars on steep hills.)

PASS if the note answers all four, each with at least these details: 1, Albert Rowe riding to work; 2, men who learned in their youth, or the 1908 run's average age of 58; 3, the step above the back wheel; 4, being thrown over the handlebars head first.
FAIL if any of the four is missing or answered without its detail. Name the question that fails.

Passages outside the limits, such as penny-farthings being everyday transport from about 1875 to 1885, or American trick riders, don't count as answers here and don't matter to this check.
