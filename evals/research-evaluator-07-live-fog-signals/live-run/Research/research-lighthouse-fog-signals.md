# Research - Lighthouse Fog Signals

## Summary

From about 1900, compressed-air signals (the siren, the diaphone, and later the tyfon) became the main powered fog signals, with the siren and the diaphone the most widely used.[^1] In the 1950s, British and Irish stations in the findings ran diaphones, sirens with large trumpets, other compressed-air horns, and explosive signals.[^4][^8][^12][^17][^26] All three authorities appear. Trinity House had a siren and trumpet at Trevose Head, diaphones fitted at Souter Point in 1953, and an explosive signal at Beachy Head.[^4][^8][^10] The Commissioners of Irish Lights ran explosive signals at stations such as Rathlin East, Mizen Head, Loop Head, and Tuskar Rock, and diaphones at stations such as Baily and Inishtrahull.[^12][^14][^15][^16][^17][^18] The Northern Lighthouse Board ran compressed-air horns fed by compressors that Kelvin diesel engines drove.[^21][^26]

A diaphone made its sound with a slotted piston that oscillated about 90 times a second, releasing rapid puffs of air into a horn and ending each note with a falling "grunt."[^2] The big compressed-air signals needed engines, compressors, and air tanks.[^1] Explosive signals were charges fired above the lantern, electrically in later designs.[^1]

Keepers kept a fog watch around the clock so they were ready to start the signal when visibility dropped.[^31] When fog came, a second keeper was called out, and in long fogs keepers doubled up, so one ran the signal while another tended the light, and a keeper could work a 16-hour day.[^32][^33] Engine stations typically had three engines so one could be serviced while another ran and a third stood ready.[^33] Part of the daylight fog watch went on cleaning and maintaining the machinery.[^34]

## Fog signal types in the 1950s

### Compressed-air signals in general

Britannica describes compressed-air signals (siren, diaphone, tyfon) as the main powered fog signals from about 1900, and names the siren and the diaphone as the most widely used.[^1] Its account is a general history, not one specific to Britain in the 1950s.[^1] The largest diaphones could be heard about eight nautical miles away in good conditions, but they needed a large compressor plant and air tanks.[^1] The tyfon came later. It uses a metal diaphragm moved by air pressure and was more compact and efficient than the earlier designs.[^1] The only tyfon in the findings at a British station is the one that replaced Ailsa Craig's signals in 1966.[^26]

### Diaphones

**How it worked.** A diaphone has a slotted piston that moves back and forth inside a slotted cylinder. Air admitted to the piston makes it oscillate about 90 times a second. Each time the slots line up, a puff of air escapes into the horn, and the result is a rapid series of puffs.[^2][^3] The diaphone works on the siren's principle, but with the piston moving in a cylinder with matching ports.[^1]

**What it sounded like.** The diaphone's distinctive low "grunt" at the end of a note comes from the piston slowing down when the drive air is cut off, so the pitch falls.[^2] The "Type F" diaphone gave a tone of about 250 Hz and was widely used at lighthouses.[^2][^3]

**Power.** Diaphones ran on compressed air from a compressor plant.[^1] An Irish Lights heritage page says a diaphone needed a large diesel compressor engine, which had to keep making air because the diaphone kept using it.[^33]

**Trinity House: Souter Point (Tyne and Wear).** One page says Stone-Chance diaphone fog signals were fitted here in 1953 and sound through exponential horns. It gives the signal as one 5-second blast every 30 seconds, with a range of about 2 nautical miles, and says Trinity House closed the signal and lighthouse in 1988.[^8] The same page says the building dates from 1871 and first used compressed-air horns, which were swapped for sirens in 1879 and for more powerful units in 1919.[^8] A local heritage site lists a diaphone fog signal among the station's features.[^9]

**Commissioners of Irish Lights.** Baily (Howth, Dublin) changed from a siren to a G type diaphone in 1926.[^17] Inishtrahull's 1958 station was built with the diaphone fog signal in the structure above the light.[^18] Dún Laoghaire East Pier installed a diaphone in 1944.[^19] Rathlin West's diaphone was replaced by an electric signal on 17 April 1982.[^20]

### Sirens and trumpets

**Trinity House: Trevose Head (Cornwall).** Work on a new fog signal began in autumn 1911, as part of alterations to the keepers' dwellings.[^4] The horn was a large rectangular trumpet 36 feet long, designed by Lord Rayleigh, Trinity House's scientific adviser.[^4][^7] Trinity House says the trumpet stayed in place until 1963, which puts it in service through the 1950s.[^4]

### Northern Lighthouse Board horns, engines, and compressors

**Sumburgh Head (Shetland).** Three K-series Kelvin diesel engines were installed in spring 1952, replacing older Crossley engines that ran on paraffin.[^21][^22] The Kelvin diesels powered the compressors, and the horn was controlled by an air-driven clockwork mechanism.[^21][^22] An NLB technician said the engines went in during the early 1950s and were not the originals.[^23] The signal gave one blast every 90 seconds.[^25] It fell silent in 1987.[^21][^24]

**A standard NLB arrangement.** St Abb's Head's engine house held Kelvin diesel engines driving Alley & McClellan air compressors that supplied the foghorn.[^26][^27] A Historic Environment Scotland photo record calls Kelvin diesels driving Alley and McLellan compressors a standard NLB arrangement.[^26][^28]

**Tiumpan Head (Lewis).** Three Kelvin engines and compressors served the fog signal. While it sounded, two were in service to hold the air pressure and one stood by, in rotation. The signal was discontinued in 1984.[^26]

**Ailsa Craig.** Two fog signals in horn houses at opposite ends of the island were built in 1883 to 1886 and run from a central gas supply. A Tyfon system near the tower replaced them in 1966.[^26][^29] Another record gives the pressure as up to 75 psi.[^29]

### Explosive signals

**How they worked.** Britannica says cannon came first. Later, explosive charges were attached to retractable booms above the lantern and detonated electrically. Some charges included magnesium for a bright flash. They could be heard about four nautical miles away.[^1]

**Trinity House: Beachy Head (East Sussex).** An explosive jib on the lantern roof was installed in 1902 and stayed in service into the 1950s and beyond; the sources differ on when it ended (see Disagreement / Uncertainty).[^10] One source calls Beachy Head one of the last lighthouses still using explosive signals.[^10] Trinity House also developed an electrically fired gun-cotton charge, "Signal, Fog, Mk I," which needed hand recharging after each blast.[^10]

**Commissioners of Irish Lights.** Rathlin East fired a double tonite explosion every 5 minutes. This signal replaced a fog gun in 1918, gained a flash of light in darkness from September 1965, and was discontinued in 1972 "for security reasons," along with similar signals around the coast.[^12][^13] Mizen Head's explosive signal was fired at intervals. Armed men raided the station on 21 May 1920 and took nearly all the explosives, and the Board then withdrew explosive signals from all stations around the coast. The Mizen Head signal was re-established on 29 February 1924 and finally withdrawn in 1969.[^14] Loop Head's explosive signal was discontinued in 1972.[^15] Tuskar Rock had an explosive signal from 1879, when it replaced a bell, until 1974, when a temporary low-power diaphone took over.[^16]

### How common each type was

Britannica names the siren and the diaphone as the most widely used compressed-air signals.[^1] The Type F diaphone was widely used at lighthouses.[^2] Kelvin diesels driving Alley and McLellan compressors were a standard NLB arrangement.[^28] The largest diaphones needed a large compressor plant and air tanks, which tied them to stations that could house that machinery.[^1] The 1972 Irish Lights withdrawal covered explosive signals "around the coast," which shows they were in use at more stations than the ones named here.[^12]

## How keepers ran and maintained the signals

The sources describe fog signal work in general, mostly for compressed-air, engine-driven signals.[^31][^32][^33]

### Deciding when to start

The Northern Lighthouse Board says keepers mainly maintained the light and the fog signal, and kept a fog watch throughout each 24 hours so as to be ready to run the fog signal in poor visibility.[^31] A keepers' history site says the keeper started and tended the fog signal when conditions called for it, and that stations with a fog signal kept a fog watch in daylight.[^34]

### Starting, keeping the rhythm, and stopping

**Engines.** On an engine-driven compressed-air signal, starting the engines took two people. Running engines needed constant lubrication and attention, and the engine had to keep making air because the diaphone kept using it.[^33]

**Rhythm.** At Sumburgh Head an air-driven clockwork mechanism operated valves in sequence and at the right time to release air into the horn. This was how the rhythm was kept.[^26] The signal patterns recorded for individual stations include one 5-second blast every 30 seconds at Souter Point, one blast every 90 seconds at Sumburgh Head, and a double explosion every 5 minutes at Rathlin East.[^8][^25][^12]

**Explosive charges.** Trinity House's electrically fired gun-cotton charge, "Signal, Fog, Mk I," needed hand recharging after each blast.[^10]

### Sharing the work

**The watch system.** A Trinity House Senior Principal Keeper describes the day split into four watches. When fog came, the keeper on watch in the lantern called out the man he had relieved to take a fog watch. That extra watch came out of off-duty time. In daylight the keeper on watch ran the fog signal himself.[^32]

**Doubling up.** An Irish Lights heritage page says the three keepers on duty kept four-hour watches in turn. Once fog set in they doubled up, with one running the signal while another tended the light, so a keeper could work a 16-hour day in prolonged fog.[^33]

**A second keeper at night.** At some stations a second keeper was needed on night watch in fog, because the keeper on light duty could not also run the fog signal compressor. The same source says semi-automatic operation was reducing the problem.[^34]

**Engines in rotation.** Stations normally had three engines, so one could be serviced while another ran and a third stood ready in case the running one failed.[^33] At Tiumpan Head, two engines and compressors were in service to hold the air pressure while one stood by, in rotation.[^26]

**Staffing built in for fog.** Rock stations such as Bell Rock and Skerryvore had six lightkeepers, and mainland fog signal stations had four.[^31] At Inishowen, the 1900 siren fog signal came with a third, two-storey dwelling for two additional assistant keepers needed for fog signal duties.[^35] A Canmore entry says an NLB station upgrade at the start of the twentieth century added an engine room for the air compressors, a radio room, and an additional keeper to operate the foghorn.[^28] These are records of the staffing built into fog signal stations, not of the 1950s rota.[^35][^28]

### Maintenance and training

**Daily upkeep.** At stations with a fog signal, part of the daylight fog watch went on cleaning and maintaining the machinery.[^34] Running engines needed constant lubrication and attention.[^33]

**Getting to the horn.** At St Abb's Head the horn had sheltering steps that gave access to it for maintenance. At Butt of Lewis an iron handrail at the top of the foghorn tower let keepers maintain the horn safely in bad weather.[^26][^28]

**Training.** A Trinity House keeper's training included practical instruction in maintaining fog signal equipment at an engine station.[^32]

### What could go wrong

**Engine failure.** The third, standby engine was kept ready in case the running one failed.[^33]

**Weather at the horn.** The handrail at Butt of Lewis existed so keepers could work on the horn safely in bad weather.[^28]

**Theft of explosives.** At Mizen Head, armed men raided the station in 1920 and took nearly all the explosives, after which the Board withdrew explosive signals from all stations for a time.[^14] Irish Lights discontinued its remaining explosive signals in 1972 "for security reasons."[^12]

**Fatigue.** In prolonged fog a keeper could work a 16-hour day.[^33]

## Disagreement / Uncertainty

**Contested claim: when Beachy Head's explosive signal ended.** Worldwide Lighthouses says it stayed in service until the 1980s, when an electric horn replaced it. A search-tool summary of another source gives 1976 and an "ELG 500" electric emitter as the replacement.[^10] Either date puts the signal in use through the 1950s. The official listing extract stopped before its description, so it confirms neither the jib nor the fog signal.[^11]

**Contested claim: the diaphone's grunt.** The sources differ on whether the grunt follows a high tone or is a separate low note.[^2][^3]

**Contested claim: the Tuskar Rock dates.** The Lighthouse Digest entry gives an explosive signal until 1974 and a temporary diaphone after it. Another source in the same search gave 2011 for the end of Irish fog signals, and Tuskar's own record says the signal was disestablished on 11 January 2001.[^16]

**Single-source claims:**
- The maker's name "Stone-Chance" for the Souter Point diaphones rests on one hobby-site page.[^8]
- A range of 15 to 20 miles for the diaphone comes from one source and was not cross-checked.[^2][^3]
- The Ailsa Craig signals running from a central gas supply is unconfirmed, and the 75 psi figure comes from a record of unclear identity.[^29]
- Trinity House's "Signal, Fog, Mk I" comes from search-tool summaries that trace to Wikipedia's Foghorn article.[^10]

**Numbers that differ:**
- **Trevose trumpet in service:** autumn 1911 start of work and in place until 1963[^4]; 1913 to 1964[^5]; 1913 to 1963[^6].
- **Diaphone range:** about eight nautical miles for the largest diaphones[^1]; 15 to 20 miles[^2]; about 2 nautical miles for Souter Point's signal[^8].
- **Watches:** four watches a day at a Trinity House station[^32]; four-hour watches kept in turn by three keepers on duty, on an Irish Lights page[^33].

## Useful Details

- The Rathlin East explosive signal gained a flash of light in darkness from September 1965.[^12]
- Plans for renewing the fog signal and generators at St Abb's Head survive from 1934.[^26]
- Alan Renton's book "Lost Sounds: The Story of Fog Signals" (Whittles Publishing) traces fog signals from early percussion instruments through compressed-air sirens and diaphones to electric emitters. It draws on documents held by lighthouse authorities, museums, and archives.[^30]
