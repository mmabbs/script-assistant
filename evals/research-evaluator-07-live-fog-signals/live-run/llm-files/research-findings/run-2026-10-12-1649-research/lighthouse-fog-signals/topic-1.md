[reported] Compressed-air signals (siren, diaphone, tyfon) became the main powered fog signals from about 1900. Britannica says the siren and the diaphone were the most widely used. The diaphone works on the siren's principle but uses a slotted piston moving in a cylinder with matching ports. The largest diaphones could be heard about eight nautical miles in good conditions, but they needed a large compressor plant and air tanks. The tyfon came later. It uses a metal diaphragm moved by air pressure and was more compact and efficient than the earlier designs. Britannica's text is a general history, not specific to Britain in the 1950s.

- https://britannica.com/technology/lighthouse/Sound-signals (no date seen). Encyclopedia with a general scope. Content was read from search-tool extracts, because the page could not be opened directly.

[reported] How a diaphone makes its sound. A slotted piston moves back and forth inside a slotted cylinder. Air admitted to the piston makes it oscillate about 90 times a second. Each time the slots line up, a puff of air escapes into the horn, and the result is a rapid series of puffs. The distinctive low "grunt" at the end of a note comes from the piston slowing down when the drive air is cut off, so the pitch falls. The "Type F" diaphone gave a tone of about 250 Hz and was widely used at lighthouses. One source gives its audible range as 15 to 20 miles. This is a single claim and was not cross-checked. The sources differ on whether the grunt follows a high tone or is a separate low note.

[verified] Commissioners of Irish Lights, explosive signals in the 1950s. Rathlin East lit an explosive fog signal of a double tonite explosion every 5 minutes. It replaced a fog gun in 1918, gained a flash of light in darkness from September 1965, and was discontinued in 1972 "for security reasons" along with similar signals around the coast. Irish Lights' station page and its factsheet both give the 1972 date. Mizen Head's explosive signal was fired at intervals. Armed men raided the station on 21 May 1920 and took nearly all the explosives, and the Board then withdrew explosive signals from all stations around the coast. The Mizen Head signal was re-established on 29 February 1924 and finally withdrawn in 1969. Loop Head's explosive signal was discontinued in 1972. Tuskar Rock had an explosive signal from 1879, when it replaced a bell, until 1974, when a temporary low-power diaphone took over. The Irish sources do not say who raided the station, and they do not explain the 1972 "security" reason.

[verified] Northern Lighthouse Board, engines and compressors at Sumburgh Head (Shetland). Three K-series Kelvin diesel engines were installed in spring 1952, replacing the older Crossley engines, which ran on paraffin. The horn is controlled by an air-driven clockwork mechanism, and Kelvin diesel engines power the compressors. An NLB engineer said the engines went in during the early 1950s and were not the originals. The fog signal fell silent in 1987. The Chance Heritage Trust entry gives the signal as one blast every 90 seconds. The sources I could open do not name the Sumburgh signal's horn type. I did not confirm it as a diaphone.

[reported] Northern Lighthouse Board, other stations. St Abb's Head's engine house held Kelvin diesel engines driving Alley & McClellan air compressors that supplied the foghorn, and plans for renewing its fog signal and generators survive from 1934. A Historic Environment Scotland photo record says Kelvin diesels driving Alley and McLellan compressors were a standard NLB arrangement. At Tiumpan Head (Lewis), three Kelvin engines and compressors served the fog signal. While it sounded, two were in service to hold the air pressure and one stood by, in rotation. That signal was discontinued in 1984. At Ailsa Craig, two fog signals in horn houses at opposite ends of the island were built in 1883-6 and run from a central gas supply. A Tyfon system near the tower replaced them in 1966. Another record of unclear identity gives the pressure as up to 75 psi. The Ailsa Craig gas-engine detail is unconfirmed. Canmore also says the earliest warnings at some NLB stations were cliff-top bells, replaced later by compressed-air foghorns.

[reported] Bells. Britannica says bell strikers were driven either by weight-driven clockwork or by a piston powered by compressed gas, usually carbon dioxide.
