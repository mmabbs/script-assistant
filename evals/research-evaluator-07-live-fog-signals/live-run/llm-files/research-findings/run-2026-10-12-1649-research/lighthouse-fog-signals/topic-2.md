[verified] Fog watch and the extra watch in fog. The Northern Lighthouse Board's history page says keepers mainly maintained the light and the fog signal, and that they kept a fog watch throughout each 24 hours so as to be ready to run the fog signal in poor visibility. The same page says rock stations such as Bell Rock and Skerryvore had six lightkeepers and mainland fog signal stations had four. A Trinity House Senior Principal Keeper's narrative describes the day split into four watches. When fog came, the keeper on watch in the lantern called out the man he had relieved to take a fog watch. That extra watch came out of off-duty time, and in daylight the keeper on watch ran the fog signal himself. An Irish Lights heritage page (a mirror site, not the main Irish Lights site) says the three keepers on duty kept four-hour watches in turn. Once fog set in they doubled up, with one running the signal while another tended the light, so a keeper could work a 16-hour day in prolonged fog. These sources describe fog signals in general, mostly compressed-air engine-driven ones. None separates the routine by signal type. The pages were not opened in full. The details above come from search-tool extracts of them. None of the pages carries a date I could see, and the narrative does not give the years it covers.

[reported] Sharing the work on engine-driven compressed-air signals. The Irish Lights heritage page says a diaphone needed a large diesel compressor engine attended by the keeper on watch, and that the engine had to keep making air because the diaphone kept using it. It says starting the engines took two people, that running engines needed constant lubrication and attention, and that stations normally had three engines, so one could be serviced while another ran and a third stood ready in case the running one failed. Topic 1 found the same arrangement at an NLB station. At Tiumpan Head (Lewis) three Kelvin engines and compressors served the fog signal. While it sounded, two were in service to hold the air pressure and one stood by, in rotation. That signal was discontinued in 1984. Neither source says the three-engine arrangement was used at every station, and neither gives a servicing interval. The Irish page's account is general, and I could not place it in the 1950s. The Tiumpan point comes from Topic 1's findings, which cite the Canmore record. I saw it only in a search extract this time.

[reported] Maintenance access and station layout. Canmore records say the horn at St Abb's Head had sheltering steps that gave access to the horn for maintenance, and that at Butt of Lewis an iron handrail at the top of the foghorn tower let keepers maintain the horn safely in bad weather. At Sumburgh Head the horn is controlled by an air-driven clockwork mechanism that operates valves in sequence and at the right time to release air into the horn. This was how the rhythm was kept. Topic 1 found that Kelvin diesel engines drive the compressors there. The Canmore text does not say how often keepers climbed to the horn or what they did there.
