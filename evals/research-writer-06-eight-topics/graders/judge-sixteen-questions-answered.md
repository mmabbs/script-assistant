The plan asked sixteen questions across eight topics, and the findings answered all sixteen. Each question is listed with what the findings hold for it.

1. When was pond ice ready to cut, and how thick did it have to be? (Findings: Harvest from the middle of January into February after a hard freeze; not cut until the clear ice was at least 14 inches thick; a foreman bored test holes with an auger each morning.)
2. What tools cut the ice, and what size were the blocks? (Findings: Wyeth's horse-drawn tools (1825, patented 1829); the marker scored a grid of blocks 22 inches square; the plough deepened the grooves to about two-thirds; breaking bars, saws, and pike poles.)
3. How were the icehouses at the ponds built, and how much did they hold? (Findings: Above ground on the pond shore; double or triple wooden walls packed with sawdust or tanbark; steep vented roof, often painted white; drained floor over gravel; the largest on Fresh Pond held about 9,400 tons.)
4. How much ice did they lose while it was stored? (Findings: The 1847 ledger: about 17 percent lost between March and the last autumn sailing. A general history, with no source, says a fifth to a third, and up to half for small houses.)
5. How was the ice packed into the ship's hold? (Findings: Hold cleaned and dried; a pine-plank lining packed with sawdust (the 'ice ceiling'); a sawdust bed; blocks on edge in tight crossed layers; one ton of sawdust for every 11 tons of ice.)
6. What kept the cargo from melting or shifting at sea? (Findings: Battens and wedges against shifting; sawdust and hay on top; hatches sealed and never opened at sea; meltwater pumped from the bilge every 4 hours.)
7. Where did the ice ships sail, and how long did the voyages take? (Findings: Calcutta about 130 days by way of the Cape of Good Hope; the Caribbean two to three weeks; the southern US ports, New Orleans the largest, several weeks; also South America, Bombay, Madras, Hong Kong, and a few cargoes to Australia.)
8. How much of the cargo melted on the way? (Findings: A logbook study found an average loss of 38 percent from Boston to Calcutta; a general history says about a quarter for well-stowed ships; short routes often lost under a tenth.)
9. How were the ice houses at the far ports built, and how much did they hold? (Findings: Built before regular cargoes; Calcutta's round brick house with a domed roof held about 1,250 tons; Havana's was partly sunk into the ground; New Orleans had several on the levee; Madras's stood near the beach.)
10. Who ran them, and how was the ice sold? (Findings: Agents ran them; the Havana agent Joaquín Barcia sold by ticket; sales only in the early morning; ice sold by weight; account customers billed monthly; some New Orleans houses delivered by cart.)
11. Who cut and handled the ice, and how were the crews organised? (Findings: Local farmers at first, Irish immigrants from the 1840s, later French-Canadians; labourers hired by the day, skilled men for the season, a foreman over each crew; about 75 men on the largest ponds, in gangs.)
12. What were the working conditions, and what were the dangers? (Findings: Cold and frostbite; long days and night work by lantern; injuries from saws, bars, and falling blocks; falling through the ice; nine drownings among harvest crews on the Cambridge ponds between 1850 and 1860.)
13. Who bought the ice at the far ports? (Findings: In Calcutta, British residents, wealthy Indian merchants, hospitals, clubs, and messes; in Havana, cafés, confectioners, and wealthy households; in New Orleans, bars, hotels, households, the food trades, and river steamboats.)
14. What did the ice cost there? (Findings: Calcutta 3 annas a pound in the 1830s and 1840s; New Orleans about 2½ cents a pound by 1860, after a steady fall; prices rose and fell with ship arrivals; Havana had a government-fixed maximum.)
15. What ended the trade? (Findings: The Civil War cut off the South; ice machines (Gorrie, Carré, Harrison); New Orleans's first factory in 1868 made about 12 tons a day; factory ice became cheaper; Indian factories from the late 1870s; steamships and the Suez Canal; the growing home market; fouled ponds.)
16. When did the last cargoes sail? (Findings: The last recorded cargo to Calcutta sailed in 1882 aboard the bark Margaret Ellery. Sources disagree on when the sea trade ended altogether: the early 1880s, or Caribbean cargoes into the 1890s.)
