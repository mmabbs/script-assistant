# Research - Stained Glass Windows

## Summary

Medieval glaziers coloured glass while it was molten, using metal oxides, and cut it with a hot iron before trimming the edges with a grozing iron.[^1][^3] Painted detail was fired onto the glass.[^4] The pieces were joined with H-shaped lead strips, and iron bars carried the weight of the finished panels.[^5]

## Making the coloured glass

Colour went into the glass while it was still molten. Glaziers added metal oxides to the batch: cobalt for blue, copper for red.[^1] Red made this way came out so dark it was nearly black.[^1] The answer was "flashed" red, a sheet of clear glass carrying a thin layer of red glass on its surface.[^1][^2]

To cut a piece, the glazier drew a red-hot iron along a line marked on the glass, and the glass cracked along it. The edge was then trimmed with a grozing iron, a flat bar with a notch at one end used to nibble the glass to shape. Diamond cutters came much later.[^3]

Faces, the folds of clothing, and lettering were painted on in grisaille, a brown-black paint of ground glass and iron oxide. Each painted piece was fired in a kiln so the paint fused to the glass.[^2][^4]

## Building the window

The pieces were held in strips of lead with an H-shaped cross-section, called cames. The glass sat in the channels of the H, and the joints were soldered. A typical lancet window held about 800 pieces of glass.[^5][^6]

Before any glass was cut, the glazier drew the whole design full size on a whitewashed wooden table, then cut the pieces and laid them out over the drawing. When a panel was done, the table was washed and whitened again for the next design.[^7]

Iron bars, called armatures, ran across the window opening and carried the weight of the leaded panels.[^5]

## Disagreement / Uncertainty

The piece count of about 800 comes from a survey of one cathedral's lancets and may not hold elsewhere.[^6]
