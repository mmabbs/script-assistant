## Penny-farthings in everyday use, and their riders

In April 1908 the Coventry Evening Herald printed a photograph of Albert Rowe, a watchmaker, riding his penny-farthing to his workshop on Hertford Street. The caption said he rode it there every working day.[^1]

The penny-farthing riders of these years were mostly men who had learned on the machine in their youth. The Ordinary Riders' Club, a club for riders of the old high-wheelers, held an annual run through Coventry. Its 1908 run had 14 riders, and their average age was 58.[^2][^5]

One finding lies outside the 1905 to 1914 period asked about: penny-farthings were everyday transport on English roads only from about 1875 to 1885.[^6] In the late 1880s the safety bicycle, with two wheels of the same size and a chain driving the back wheel, replaced them, and by 1892 no English maker still offered a high-wheeler in its catalogue.[^6]

## Riding a penny-farthing

A rider mounted from behind. Holding the handlebars, he set his left foot on a small step fixed to the frame just above the back wheel, pushed off with his right foot to get the machine rolling, then stood up on the step and slid forward into the saddle above the front wheel. To get off, he did the same in reverse, finding the step with his left foot as the machine slowed.[^3][^4]

The rider sat almost directly above the hub of the front wheel. A stone or a rut that stopped the wheel could throw him over the handlebars head first, a fall riders called "taking a header." A 1908 handbook told riders to hook their legs over the handlebars when going down steep hills, so that a fall would land them feet first.[^3]
