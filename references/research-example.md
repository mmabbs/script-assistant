# Research - Pony Express Mail

## Summary

The Pony Express was a private horseback relay that carried letters, telegrams, and newspapers between St. Joseph, Missouri, and Sacramento, California, with a steamer covering the last leg to San Francisco.[^2] It ran from April 3, 1860, into November 1861.[^1][^2] The Central Overland California & Pike's Peak Express Company ran it, a firm set up by the freighting partners Russell, Majors & Waddell.[^2][^3]

Home stations every 75 to 100 miles housed riders. Relay stations every 10 to 15 miles kept fresh horses.[^2] The mail rode in a mochila, a leather cover with locked pockets that sat over the saddle and moved to each fresh horse.[^7]

The service never made money.[^6][^8] The company was in debt before it began, did not win the federal mail contract its owner had counted on, and was replaced by a government-backed transcontinental telegraph in October 1861.[^2][^9] The company kept few or no records, so counts of stations, riders, and letters differ, and several famous stories are disputed.[^6][^11]

## The route and the stations

### The route

The line ran nearly 2,000 miles through present-day Kansas, Nebraska, the northeast corner of Colorado, Wyoming, Utah, Nevada, and California.[^9] One encyclopedia gives 1,966 miles, by way of Fort Kearny, the Platte River valley, South Pass, and Salt Lake City.[^5] St. Joseph was the westernmost point the railroad and telegraph reached.[^8] At Sacramento, westbound mail went to San Francisco on a paddle steamer.[^2]

### The five divisions

Alexander Majors split the route into five divisions, numbered east to west: St. Joseph to Fort Kearny; on to Horseshoe Station near Fort Laramie; on to Fort Bridger and the Salt Lake Valley; across the Great Basin to Roberts Creek Station in today's Nevada; and across the Nevada desert and the Sierra Nevada to Sacramento, the hardest leg.[^2]

### Setting up the stations

The company announced the service on January 27, 1860.[^3] It then had 67 days to hire riders, station keepers, and mail handlers and to stock stations, some not yet built or even located.[^2]

Spacing followed the fact that a healthy horse could typically gallop 8 to 12 miles.[^3] In the east the company used existing stage stations and road ranches. In the remote west it built new relay stations.[^3] Home stations were often stage stations, hotels, or large ranches.[^3]

The network grew from 86 stations on the first run to 147 by mid-1861.[^2] Some went up late: in Nevada, Camp Station and Sulphur Springs Station were built around July 1861 to split long stretches.[^4] In the western Nevada desert, relay stations sat at water sources the Paiute people depended on.[^12]

### Who ran a station

Station keepers and stock tenders timed each change of horse and rider so no time was lost.[^9] Hollenberg Station in Kansas was a converted road ranch: a long, narrow five-room building where Gerat Hollenberg sold supplies, meals, and lodging, and his wife Sophia fed travelers. In 1860 and 1861 it gave riders and horses food and shelter.[^13]

Richard Burton, who traveled the route in those years, described Nevada's "Robber's Roost" station as having a mud floor strewn with ashes and meat scraps, a leaking roof, and "portholes" for windows.[^6]

### The first run

On April 3, 1860, the westbound rider left St. Joseph at 7:15 p.m., reportedly with five telegrams, 49 letters, and newspapers.[^2] The eastbound mail left San Francisco by steamer for Sacramento. There Sam Hamilton took it east and handed it to Warren Upson, who crossed the Sierra Nevada in a blizzard. The eastbound mail reached St. Joseph on April 13; the westbound reached San Francisco about 1 a.m. on April 14.[^2]

### Schedule and price

The service ran once a week each way at first, and twice from July 1860.[^2] It charged $5 per half-ounce, later $1.[^2][^7] Its best customers were businesses, governments, and news organizations.[^7] While the telegraph was being built in 1861, the Pony carried telegrams only between the two advancing ends of the wire.[^1]

## The riders and the ride

### Hiring

A *Sacramento Union* ad of March 19, 1860, sought "Men familiar with the management of horses, as hostlers or riders" at $50 a month.[^2] The NPS says hires ranged from teenagers to about 40 and had to weigh under 120 pounds.[^2] An encyclopedia gives the average age as nineteen.[^5] The company employed 80 to 100 riders and several hundred station workers.[^2]

### The oath

Employees swore not to use profane language, get drunk, gamble, or treat animals cruelly.[^2] The Postal Service historian says riders swore it on a Bible before being hired.[^8]

### The load

A rider carried 20 pounds of mail and 25 pounds of equipment.[^2] Letters were written on very thin paper, and the saddles, mostly made at Israel Landis' shop in St. Joseph, were built light.[^5][^7]

### The mochila

The mochila was a leather cover with four pockets, called cantinas. Openings let it fit over the saddle horn and cantle, and the rider sat on it.[^7] Each packet of letters was wrapped in oiled silk.[^7] The pockets were locked. Per the National Postal Museum, three opened only at the military posts at Forts Kearny, Laramie, Bridger, and Churchill and at Salt Lake City. The fourth, which any station master could open, held a time card for arrival and departure times.[^7]

### The handoff

At each change the rider snatched the mochila off one saddle and draped it over the next.[^7] A rider usually covered 75 to 100 miles, then handed the mail to the next rider at a home station.[^3][^7] The NPS says the exchange was more casual than legend has it; riders often stopped to eat or drink.[^2] Mark Twain, who saw a rider from a stagecoach in 1861, wrote that the transfer "was made in the twinkling of an eye."[^2][^6]

### Horses and speed

The company bought 400 to 500 horses, thoroughbreds for the east and California mustangs for the west.[^2] Horses averaged 10 miles per hour and at times galloped up to 25.[^2] One magazine says riders rarely galloped, since prairie-dog holes could break a horse's leg.[^6] The mail moved 24 hours a day, year-round.[^4][^5] Lincoln's inaugural address of March 1861 made the fastest run, seven days and seventeen hours.[^2][^8][^9]

### Hazards

Riders interviewed late in life remembered the cold and the danger of losing the trail at night in winter, not outlaws.[^11][^6] One Utah rider, Thomas Owen King, blackened his face with gunpowder against snow blindness.[^6] Snow between Salt Lake and Fort Laramie often beat a single rider, but January deliveries ran only four to six days late.[^2]

### The Pyramid Lake War

The 1859 silver strikes brought miners onto Paiute land in Nevada.[^12] On May 7, 1860, an incident at Williams Station set off the Pyramid Lake War. On May 21 a station keeper was killed and Simpson Park Station burned. In June the company cancelled service between Carson City and Salt Lake City; with federal troops, mail to California resumed by July. The delays cost around $75,000, and raids returned in August.[^12] Robert "Pony Bob" Haslam rode some 400 miles across Nevada without relief during the war.[^6]

## Why it closed

### A company already in trouble

William Russell saw the Pony as a way to promote his firm and win the federal mail contract for the central route. His partners were less sure.[^2] Russell, Majors & Waddell had lived on loans against its government debts since 1858 and was "essentially bankrupt" when it launched the Pony company.[^2]

### No contract

Congress adjourned in June 1860 without acting.[^2] Russell then secretly borrowed bonds through a friend in a government agency. The story broke in December 1860 and he was arrested. He beat the embezzlement charges, but the scandal was a disaster for the Pony.[^2]

In 1861, after Texas seceded and the southern routes closed, Congress funded the overland mail, and the NPS says the contract went to Butterfield's Overland Mail Company.[^2] The Postal Service historian says the Pony "began operating under contract as a mail route" on July 1, 1861, already deeply in debt.[^8]

### The telegraph

On June 16, 1860, about ten weeks after the first run, Congress passed a bill to subsidize a telegraph line from the Missouri River to the Pacific.[^1][^9] The line was finished on October 24, 1861.[^9] On October 26 San Francisco was in direct contact with New York, and the Pony Express officially ended.[^1][^9] The last letters on the route finished their journey in November.[^1] The NPS brochure dates the last run to November 20.[^2]

### The money

Even at $5 per half-ounce, against ordinary postage of no more than ten cents, the company did not cover its operating costs.[^8] One encyclopedia puts the loss at about $200,000 and says it pushed Russell, Majors & Waddell into bankruptcy.[^5] Gerat Hollenberg lost hundreds of dollars when the parent company failed.[^13] The NPS also names delivery delays and the lack of federal help.[^12] The *Sacramento Bee*'s farewell in fall 1861 said the Pony was beaten only by "a senseless, soulless thing that eats not, sleeps not, tires not."[^11]

## Disagreement / Uncertainty

### Contested claim: the "orphans preferred" advertisement

The ad ("Wanted: Young, skinny, wiry fellows not over eighteen... Orphans preferred.") is said to have run in a California paper in spring 1860. *American Heritage* says no such notice seems to have run anywhere, and its earliest source appears to be a 1920s *Sunset* magazine writer.[^6] *Western Horseman* says nobody has confirmed where it came from, that "orphans" was first tied to the riders in a 1923 *Sunset* article, and that little evidence shows the riders were orphans.[^10] A historian quoted by NPR calls much of the lore "fakelore" from a company that "kept no records whatsoever."[^11]

### Contested claim: whether Buffalo Bill Cody rode

The Library of Congress lists Cody among the famous riders.[^9] The NPS says he never rode.[^2] *American Heritage* calls it "highly improbable."[^6]

### Contested claim: the first westbound rider

Johnny Fry is usually named, but a St. Joseph paper of April 4, 1860, named Billy Richardson.[^10] *American Heritage* says there is still no agreement.[^6]

### Contested claim: whether riders were armed

*American Heritage* says the firm did not issue firearms.[^6] *Western Horseman* says riders carried an 1851 Navy Colt pistol.[^10]

### Plausible interpretation: how widely the oath was used

The NPS and the Postal Service present it as a condition of hiring.[^2][^8] A St. Joseph Museum researcher doubts every agent out west knew of it.[^10]

### Numbers that differ

- **Stations:** 86 growing to 147[^2]; 182[^5]; about 190[^6]; about 165[^11].
- **Relay spacing:** 10 to 15 miles[^2]; 12 to 15[^4]; 10 to 12[^6].
- **Riders:** 80 at the start[^3]; 80 to 100[^2]; about 200[^5][^9]. A list of 180 likely riders was compiled from census records and memoirs, since company records don't exist.[^10]
- **Weight limit:** under 120 pounds[^2]; under 135[^10].
- **Pay:** $50 a month in the 1860 ad[^2]; $100 to $125, up to $150 on the worst routes[^9].
- **Mail carried:** more than 33,000 pieces over 600,000 miles[^2]; 34,753 over 650,000[^5].
- **Delivery times:** ten days in summer, twelve to sixteen in winter[^9]; twelve and fourteen[^5].
- **Telegraph completion:** October 24, 1861[^9]; October 26[^5].
- **Mochila pockets:** three for mail and one for a time card[^7]; mail in all four[^2].

## Useful Details

- The first westbound newspapers were printed on paper "as airy and thin as gold leaf."[^2]
- Employees joked that the initials C.O.C. & P.P. stood for "Clean Out of Cash & Poor Pay."[^2]

## Open Research Questions

- Under whose contract did the Pony carry mail from July 1, 1861? The Postal Service and the NPS describe the 1861 contract differently, and no source found explains how they fit.
- Is there a source for the story of a mochila carried off by a riderless horse in July 1860 and recovered two years later?
- Do the station counts measure the same thing: stations at launch, stations ever built, or stations including those added in 1861?
- How did the company's losses add up? The $75,000 war figure and the roughly $200,000 total come from different sources, and none breaks the total down.

## Reliability / Limitations

- `NPS, "A Brief History"`: Short official page. No citations.
- `NPS trail brochure on npshistory.com`: The fullest source here. NPS text, but read on npshistory.com, not nps.gov, with no publication date shown.
- `NPS, "The Crowds Cheered On..." (PDF)`: Trail bulletin made with the Bureau of Land Management. No citations.
- `NPS, "Rugged Men, Rigorous Rides" (PDF)`: Bulletin for one 53-mile Nevada segment. Used only for its station dates and riding hours.
- `Encyclopedia of the Great Plains`: Signed entry by historian William E. Lass. Cites the 1994 NPS Historic Resource Study. Its October 26 telegraph date conflicts with the Library of Congress.
- `American Heritage, "The Pony Rides Again and Again"`: Magazine feature with no byline in the fetched text. Quotes Majors, Twain, and Burton but gives no citation for the orphans-ad claim.
- `National Postal Museum, "Pony Express Mochila"`: Smithsonian object page. The mochila shown is a reproduction. Only source for the locked-pocket rules.
- `USPS Historian (PDF, 2008)`: Short official summary. No citations.
- `Library of Congress, "Today in History - October 24"`: Short page, no citations. Lists Cody as a rider, against the NPS.
- `Western Horseman (2006)`: Popular magazine feature quoting Christopher Corbett and St. Joseph Museum staff. Details it marks "some stories say" are left out here.
- `NPR (2010)`: Journalism quoting historians. Its station count and pay range come from photo captions.
- `NPS, "The Pyramid Lake War"`: Official page, no citations. Only source for the $75,000 figure.
- `Kansas Historical Society`: State agency entry. Only its 1857 to 1861 facts are used.
